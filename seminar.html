Machine learning for lattice gauge fixing and expensive fermionic observables

Reported4–9 Aug 2026CCP 2026
Landau gaugeWilson linesHutchinson traceBias correction

Two independent uses of machine learning, each designed so the network can only speed things up, never change the answer: a gauge-covariant network gives conventional gauge fixing a head start, and a cheap observable predicts expensive Dirac traces with an exact correction for regression bias.

Why gauge fixing is slow

Gauge-equivalent configurations describe the same physics, but propagators and other gauge-dependent quantities require picking one representative per gauge orbit. On the lattice, Landau gauge is found by maximising

Background (added)

Standard algorithms (relaxation, overrelaxation) update one site at a time, so information spreads diffusively. The slowest modes are long wavelength, and the number of sweeps grows roughly like \(L^2\): this is critical slowing down. Fourier acceleration attacks it by preconditioning in momentum space. The ML approach is another kind of preconditioner, one that learns a good global starting transformation.

The ML preconditioner

  1. Feed the original links \(U_\mu(x)\) to the network.
  2. The network outputs a gauge transformation \(g(x)\); apply it to get \(U^{g}\), already close to Landau gauge.
  3. Finish with the conventional iterative algorithm. The final gauge condition is enforced by this step, so network errors cost iterations, not correctness.

The network is built from Wilson lines, products of links along paths of several lattice spacings. Because a Wilson line transforms only at its endpoints, combining them keeps the network gauge covariant while letting each layer see beyond nearest neighbours. Shallow networks with longer Wilson lines and deeper networks with shorter ones were compared; both enlarge the receptive field.

Why covariance matters (added)

If the input is gauge transformed, \(U\to U^{h}\), the correct output must become \(g\,h^\dagger\) so that \((U^h)^{g h^\dagger}=U^{g}\). A network that respects this cannot “memorise” a gauge, which is the underlying reason the trained model generalises.

Both diagnostics, the value of \(F_U\) and the violation \(\theta\), converged in fewer iterations than with the conventional algorithm alone.

Main result

A model trained on a small lattice transferred to a substantially larger volume and still reduced the iteration count. The network learns a translation-equivariant local rule, not a per-site answer, so it is volume independent by construction.

Predicting high powers of the inverse Dirac operator

Fluctuations of the chiral condensate near a transition need derivatives of \(\ln\det M\) with respect to the quark mass, and each derivative brings another power of \(M^{-1}\):

The matrix has dimension of order \(10^7\)–\(10^9\), so traces are estimated stochastically with Hutchinson’s method:

The speaker trained a gradient-boosted decision-tree regressor \(f_\theta\) mapping the cheap \(X=\tr M^{-1}\) to the expensive \(Y=\tr M^{-n}\). Configurations were split into a labelled set (where \(Y\) is computed exactly) and an unlabelled set; the labelled set was split again into training and bias-correction subsets.

The method was tested on means, variances and higher-order quantities near the transition, and reproduced directly measured distributions over a large part of parameter space with only a small labelled fraction.

What to check

For nonlinear quantities such as a susceptibility or a Binder ratio, the bias correction must be applied to the moments that enter them, and errors should be propagated with a jackknife over the full chain including the labelled/unlabelled split. The honest benchmark is total cost at fixed final error.

Q&A

How does this compare with other fast estimators?

The speaker agreed that the right comparison is at equal accuracy and equal cost. The ML step could also sit on top of an existing approximation (for example deflation or a truncated expansion), predicting only the residual instead of replacing the method.

Does the gauge-fixing network still work when correlations extend over much longer distances?

Not yet tested in severe cases. Longer Wilson lines or a deeper network would enlarge the receptive field.

Could a Transformer or attention mechanism help?

Plausibly: attention links distant sites directly instead of passing information layer by layer. To stay gauge covariant, the attention weights must be built from gauge-invariant quantities and the values parallel transported with Wilson lines between the sites being connected.

Gradient flow for renormalization: PDF moments, sigma terms and the quark chromo-EDM

Reported4–9 Aug 2026CCP 2026
Gradient flowPion PDFOperator mixingNeutron EDM

Flowed fields are smooth on a scale \(\sqrt{8t}\) that is physical, not tied to the lattice spacing. That single fact turns three hard lattice problems (high PDF moments, disconnected sigma terms, power-divergent mixing of the chromo-EDM) into continuum limits followed by perturbative matching.

What the flow does

Background (added)

Lüscher showed that composite operators of the flowed gauge field at \(t>0\) are finite without renormalization. Flowed fermions need exactly one extra factor, a wave-function normalization \(Z_\chi\) common to all fermion operators. Near \(t\to0\) a flowed operator is related to renormalized ones by the small-flow-time expansion

\[\mathcal O_i(t)=\sum_j c_{ij}(t,\mu)\,\mathcal O_j^{R}(\mu)+O(t),\]

with coefficients \(c_{ij}\) computable in perturbation theory when \(\sqrt{8t}\) is small compared with \(1/\Lambda_{\rm QCD}\).

Higher moments of parton distributions

Mellin moments of a PDF equal forward matrix elements of local twist-2 operators with one more derivative than the power of \(x\):

On a hypercubic lattice the rotation group shrinks to \(H(4)\). From \(\langle x^3\rangle\) on, no \(H(4)\) representation isolates the twist-2 operator from lower-dimensional ones, so mixing comes with powers \(1/a^k\) that diverge in the continuum limit. This is why direct lattice calculations stopped around \(\langle x^2\rangle\)–\(\langle x^3\rangle\).

Flow solves this by changing the order of limits. At fixed flow time the flowed operator has a continuum limit with full \(O(4)\) symmetry restored, and in the continuum there is no lower-dimensional operator with the same quantum numbers. The speaker used this to reach the fifth or sixth pion-PDF moment, depending on counting convention.

  1. Compute flowed three-point functions and form ratios at several \(a\) and \(t\).
  2. At each fixed \(t\) in physical units, extrapolate \(a\to0\).
  3. Apply the perturbative matching \(c(t,\mu)\) to convert to \(\overline{\rm MS}\).
  4. Extrapolate \(t\to0\) to remove the \(O(t)\) terms.
The flow-time window

\(t\) must be large enough that \(\sqrt{8t}\gg a\) (so lattice artefacts are small) and small enough that \(\sqrt{8t}\ll1/\Lambda_{\rm QCD}\) (so the operator is still local and perturbative matching converges). Higher moments have more derivatives and are more sensitive to over-smoothing, so the usable window narrows as \(n\) grows.

With several moments, the \(x\)-dependence of the PDF can be constrained and compared with global fits.

Strange sigma terms

A pion has no valence strange quarks, so \(\bar ss\) contributes only through a disconnected quark loop correlated with the pion two-point function. These loops are expensive and noisy. A decision-tree model predicted the expensive loop data from a small number of full calculations, in the same bias-corrected spirit as the previous talk. Ensembles used pion masses of roughly 400–600 MeV; lighter masses are the next step.

The quark chromo-electric dipole moment

On the lattice, \(\mathcal O_{\rm qCEDM}\) mixes with \(P\) with a coefficient \(\sim1/a^2\): two powers of the cutoff to make up the dimension gap. Under flow this power divergence becomes a calculable \(1/t\) dependence. Ratios of flowed \(\mathcal O_{\rm qCEDM}\) and \(P\) correlators, studied as functions of \(t\), isolate the mixing coefficient so it can be subtracted.

Determining \(Z_\chi\) directly

Earlier work used ratios precisely because \(Z_\chi\) was unknown. The new work fixes it in two schemes:

  • Ringed fields (Makino–Suzuki). Normalize the flowed field by its own vacuum expectation value, schematically \(\mathring\chi=\chi\big/\sqrt{\,t^2\langle\bar\chi\overleftrightarrow{\not\!D}\chi\rangle\,}\) up to constants, so that the normalization matches the free theory.
  • Vector-current scheme. Fix \(Z_\chi\) from correlators of the conserved vector current, whose normalization is known.

After matching, the two agree. Knowing \(Z_\chi\) means individual flowed matrix elements can be renormalized without designing a ratio for each one.

Overall message

Gradient flow is one framework for three problems: power-divergent mixing becomes a \(t\)-dependence you can fit, broken rotational symmetry is restored before matching, and a single factor \(Z_\chi\) handles all fermion normalizations.

Q&A

With \(Z_\chi\) known, can individual PDF moments be computed instead of ratios?

Yes. That was the main motivation. It still requires the full continuum, matching and \(t\to0\) sequence.

How do the ringed-field and current schemes differ?

Ringed fields fix the normalization from a vacuum expectation value of a flowed fermion bilinear with a covariant derivative. The current scheme fixes it from vector-current correlators. Different systematics, so agreement after matching is a meaningful cross-check.

Which pion masses were used for the sigma terms?

Heavy: the lightest ensemble near 400 MeV, most of the analysis near 600 MeV. Lighter masses are planned.

Context

  • Makino–Suzuki (2014), ringed fermion fields
  • Shindler and collaborators, flow for CP-violating operators

The chiral phase transition in many flavour QCD and onset of the conformal window

ReportedOwe PhilipsenGoethe University Frankfurt28 Oct, 14:00–14:45
Columbia plotTricritical scalingBulk transitionConformal hyperscalingThermoparticles

The first-order chiral transitions seen on coarse lattices shrink away as \(a\to0\). Mapping the transition in the bare parameter space \((\beta,am,N_f,N_\tau)\), the speaker’s group finds the massless transition second order for \(N_f=2\)–7, unchanged at imaginary chemical potential, and absent in the continuum for \(N_f=8\). That places the onset of the conformal window at \(7<N_f^*<8\). A final part argued that pion-like states survive above the crossover and that symmetry restoration is encoded in how Goldstone modes are damped.

1. The Columbia plot, then and now

Background: Pisarski–Wilczek 1984 (added)

For the order parameter \(\Phi\sim\bar q_Rq_L\), the anomaly term \(\det\Phi\) has degree \(N_f\). For \(N_f=2\) it is quadratic and leaves \(O(4)\); for \(N_f=3\) it is cubic and drives a first-order transition in mean field; for \(N_f\ge4\) the \(\epsilon\)-expansion finds no stable fixed point. This is the origin of the first-order corner at small masses in the textbook Columbia plot.

Coarse lattices or unimproved actions do show first-order transitions for \(N_f=2,3\). That region shrinks rapidly as \(a\to0\), and no first-order transition has been seen with improved staggered actions. The continuum picture the speaker presented (Cuteri, Philipsen, Sciarra 2021) has no lower-left first-order region at all.

2. Method: transitions in bare parameter space

Why 1 and 3 (added)

A first-order transition at coexistence gives two equal, sharp peaks at \(\pm a\): then \(\langle x^4\rangle/\langle x^2\rangle^2=a^4/a^4=1\). A crossover gives a single Gaussian, whose kurtosis is 3. A critical endpoint has a universal non-Gaussian distribution in between.

3. Where the first-order region ends

As \(am\to0\) the \(Z_2\) lines bend into tricritical points. Tricritical scaling predicts \(am_c\propto(aT-aT^{\rm tric})^{5/2}\), so \((am_c)^{2/5}\) is linear in \(aT=1/N_\tau\). This was confirmed for staggered \(N_f=3\)–7 and, with \((am_{\rm PS})^{4/5}\) since \(m_{\rm PS}^2\propto m_q\), for \(O(a)\)-improved Wilson fermions with \(N_f=3\).

  • The extrapolated tricritical points lie at \(aT^{\rm tric}\approx0.05\)–0.065, i.e. \(N_\tau^{\rm tric}\approx15\)–20 for \(N_f=5\)–7.
  • For finer lattices, \(N_\tau>N_\tau^{\rm tric}\), the chiral-limit transition is second order. The first-order region is not analytically connected to the continuum.
  • Conclusion stated by the speaker: the QCD chiral transition is second order for \(N_f=2\)–7.

Other actions support this. HotQCD’s HISQ study of \(N_f=3\) sees no first-order transition down to \(m_\pi\approx80\) MeV, and the magnetic equation of state scales like \(O(2)\), the symmetry remnant of staggered quarks at finite \(a\). Domain-wall \(N_f=3\) simulations find a crossover down to physical light-quark masses.

What the argument rests on

The conclusion depends on extrapolating \(am\to0\) with the tricritical form and on \(N_\tau^{\rm tric}\) being in the scaling regime. It fixes the order but not the universality class in the chiral limit (\(O(4)\), \(U(2)\times U(2)\) or something else), which remains open.

4. Imaginary chemical potential

Imaginary \(\mu\) has no sign problem. Repeating the Columbia-plot study at \(\mu=i\,0.81\,\pi T/3\) (D’Ambrosio, Fromm, Kaiser, Philipsen 2026) gives the same situation as \(\mu=0\). At the Roberge–Weiss value \(\mu=i\pi T/3\), stout-smeared staggered (Bonati et al. 2019) and HISQ (Bielefeld–Frankfurt 2022) studies see no first-order transition either. Dyson–Schwinger results agree.

Consequence for the critical point (from backup slides)

The chiral critical surface continues smoothly from imaginary to real \(\mu\). In the scenario of Stephanov, Rajagopal and Shuryak (1998), the QCD critical endpoint is the continuation of the light-quark \(Z_2\) line; if there is no first-order region at \(\mu=0\), that class of low-energy models is ruled out. A critical point at real \(\mu\) is not excluded, but it would require an additional critical surface.

6. Many flavours and the conformal window

With the two-loop \(\beta\)-function \(\beta_0=11-\tfrac23N_f\), \(\beta_1=102-\tfrac{38}3N_f\), a Banks–Zaks fixed point \(g_*^2=-16\pi^2\beta_0/\beta_1\) appears for \(9\le N_f\le16\). It is trustworthy only near \(N_f=16\) (\(\alpha_*\approx0.04\)); at \(N_f=12\) it already sits at \(\alpha_*\approx0.75\). Below the onset \(N_f^*\), chiral symmetry is broken at \(T=0\) and restored at a thermal transition; inside the window the massless theory is conformal in the infrared and there is no thermal transition.

The motivations given: walking-technicolour models with \(N_f\to(N_f^*)^-\); “conformalisation” of dense matter suggested by neutron-star equations of state; and the conjectured IR-conformal “third phase” of Alexandru and Horváth (see the Horváth talk below).

The usual approach computes the \(\beta\)-function with \(m_q\to0\), \(a\to0\) and \(V,1/T\to\infty\). Here the strategy is different: follow how the thermal transition approaches the continuum. Three difficulties: the conformal window has no symmetry breaking at \(m=0,T=0\); \(m=0\) cannot be simulated; and \(T=0\) needs \(N_\tau=\infty\). Everything simulable has finite \(m\) and \(T\), with explicit breaking and a restoring transition at higher \(T\).

Obstacle: an unphysical bulk phase

At strong bare coupling (small \(\beta=6/g^2\)) there is a first-order bulk transition whose position does not depend on \(N_\tau\), so it is a lattice artefact. At large \(N_f\) it gets in the way because the thermal transition, \(T=1/(aN_\tau)\), is pushed to coarse lattices. Predicted in mean field (Miransky–Yamawaki 1997) and seen in simulations; one needs \(N_\tau>8\) to separate the two.

  • \(N_f=6\) and 7: lines of fixed \(N_\tau\) (up to 22) extrapolate to a second-order transition at \(am=0\) at couplings that grow with \(N_\tau\). The continuum limit (\(a\to0\), \(\beta\to\infty\), \(N_\tau\to\infty\)) of the second-order transition exists.
  • \(N_f=8\): the fixed-\(N_\tau\) lines for \(N_\tau=10\)–22 all converge to one bare coupling \(\beta_0\) at \(am=0\). The transition does not move toward weak coupling as \(N_\tau\) grows, so no chiral transition exists in the continuum.
Why \(p=1+\gamma_m^*\) (added derivation)

In an IR-conformal theory the quark mass is the only infrared scale, so the transition temperature in lattice units obeys \(aT\propto(am)^{1/(1+\gamma_m^*)}\). With \(aT=1/N_\tau\) this gives \(am_{pc}\propto N_\tau^{-(1+\gamma_m^*)}\). At fixed \(\beta\) near \(\beta_0\), the fit gives \(am_{pc}=(\beta-\beta_0)/(C N_\tau^{\,p})\propto N_\tau^{-p}\). Comparing, \(p=1+\gamma_m^*\). In a QCD-like theory, instead, \(\beta_{pc}\) at fixed \(am\) would run to weak coupling logarithmically as \(N_\tau\) grows.

Open points

\(\gamma_m^*\approx0.69\) at \(N_f=8\) is a fitted effective exponent from bare-parameter data, and the result places \(N_f^*\) lower than most \(\beta\)-function studies (table above). Independent checks, for instance spectrum hyperscaling at \(N_f=8\), would make the case stronger.

Unlike a Breit–Wigner resonance, a thermoparticle is a thermally modified stable particle. Its propagator is frame dependent and has branch points at \(k_0=p_0\pm i\gamma\) rather than simple poles, with consequences for soft pions. A related analysis of kaons slightly below and above the crossover (Bala et al. 2024) shows the same broadening; in \(\phi^4\) theory, where there is no transition, the scalar only broadens collisionally.

In an \(O(2)\) scalar theory on the lattice (Lowdon, Philipsen 2025), a massless Goldstone thermoparticle dominates the correlator in both phases. Only its damping factor changes:

Proposition (speaker’s conjecture)

Going from \(O(2)\) to \(O(4)\), or from \(SU(2)_L\times SU(2)_R\) to \(SU(3)_L\times SU(3)_R\), changes only the number of Goldstone bosons, so the order of the transition stays the same. This was posed as an open question: is \(U(1)_A\) relevant for the order at all? The spectral reconstruction neglects the continuum part \(D_c\), which is justified only below scattering thresholds.

Conclusions as presented

Chiral transition second order up to the conformal window; order independent of imaginary chemical potential; conformal window starts at \(7<N_f^*<8\); symmetry breaking determined by the damping of Goldstone modes.

Q&A

Does the absence of first-order behaviour establish \(O(4)\) criticality?

No. The bare-parameter analysis fixes the order (second) but not the universality class in the chiral limit. Positive evidence for a class means collapse onto its scaling function, which for staggered quarks at finite \(a\) shows \(O(2)\) rather than \(O(4)\).

How does this estimate of the conformal window relate to the two-loop \(\beta\)-function?

The two-loop zero is a weak-coupling guide valid near \(N_f=16\). The lower edge needs nonperturbative input; this talk gets it from where the thermal transition loses its continuum limit, rather than from the \(\beta\)-function.

A new method for locating a tricritical point using Binder-cumulant derivatives and its application to heavy-quark QCD

PreliminaryTatsuya WadaYITP, Kyoto University28 Oct, 14:45–15:05
With Masakiyo Kitazawa and Kazuyuki Kanaya (University of Tsukuba)
Roberge–Weiss endpointBinder cumulantLogarithmic correctionsHopping-parameter expansion

On the Roberge–Weiss plane of heavy-quark QCD the endpoint changes from a triple point to a 3D-Ising point through a tricritical point. Instead of fitting Binder-cumulant crossings, the speaker reads the thermal exponent \(y_t\) directly from the ratio of \(\beta\)-derivatives of the Binder cumulant on two volumes, with logarithmic corrections built in. With volumes up to \(LT=15\) this puts the tricritical point at \(\lambda_{\rm tric}\approx0.03\).

Why heavy quarks and imaginary \(\mu\)

The goal is the QCD critical point at finite \(T\) and \(\mu\), which Monte Carlo cannot reach directly because of the sign problem. The heavy-quark corner of the Columbia plot is a controllable testing ground. At \(m_q\to\infty\) QCD is \(SU(3)\) Yang–Mills with a first-order deconfinement transition. Lowering the quark mass weakens it until it becomes a crossover; the boundary is the second-order heavy-quark critical point (Kiyohara et al. 2021; Ashikawa et al. 2024). The question is how this changes with density. At imaginary \(\hat\mu_q=\mu/T=i\pi/3\) there is no sign problem.

Roberge–Weiss periodicity

What the endpoint can be

For \(N_f=2\) heavy quarks, three lines meet at the RW endpoint: the RW line and the two deconfinement lines on either side of it (Philipsen et al. 2015, 2016). Their character depends on the quark mass:

The Binder cumulant and its three values

Deriving 1.5 and 2 (added)

Triple point. Three degenerate phases with \(\Im\Omega=+a,-a,0\), each with weight 1/3: \(\langle x^2\rangle=\tfrac23a^2\), \(\langle x^4\rangle=\tfrac23a^4\), so \(B_4=\tfrac{2/3}{4/9}=1.5\).

Tricritical point. The Landau potential starts at \(\phi^6\), so at mean field \(P(x)\propto e^{-cx^6}\) and \(\langle x^n\rangle\propto\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{n+1}6\big)\). Then \(B_4=\Gamma(\tfrac56)\Gamma(\tfrac16)/\Gamma(\tfrac12)^2=\dfrac{\pi/\sin(\pi/6)}{\pi}=2\). Three dimensions is the upper critical dimension for tricriticality, so this value is reached only up to logarithmic corrections.

3D Ising. No closed form; 1.60359(4) is known numerically.

The earlier study and its limitation

Czaban, Cuteri, Philipsen et al. (2015, 2016) used Wilson fermions with \(\kappa\sim1/(2am_q)\), \(N_t=4\) and 6, and aspect ratios \(LT=N_s/N_t\) up to about 6. Fitting \(b_4,c_4,\beta_c,y_t\) together, the fitted \(b_4\) did not match the expected values, so they relied on \(y_t\) instead. Plots of \(1/y_t\) against \(\kappa\) did move between the three plateaus (1/3, 1/2, 0.63), but with large scatter. The lesson drawn: the exponent approach is promising, but logarithmic corrections and finite-size effects must be handled.

New method: ratio of Binder derivatives

How fast the log correction fades (added)

For large \(LT\), \(\ln\!\big(\ln(sLT)/\ln LT\big)\approx\ln s/\ln LT\), so the correction term is about \(\hat y_t/\ln LT\). It decays only logarithmically, which is why going from \(LT=6\) to 15 matters, and why an unaccounted \(\hat y_t\) biases a fitted \(y_t\) in small volumes.

Simulation

  • Wilson fermions in the hopping-parameter expansion with heat-bath updates. At \(N_t=4\) the leading quark effect is a Polyakov-loop term with coupling \(\lambda=64N_fN_c\kappa^4\), so \(\lambda\) grows as the quarks get lighter (\(\lambda\propto\kappa^4\sim1/m_q^4\) at leading order). The cost is close to pure Yang–Mills.
  • Configurations generated at leading order; next-to-leading-order terms included by reweighting; multi-point reweighting for a continuous scan in \(\beta\).
  • \(N_t=4\), \(N_s=24\)–60, so \(LT=6\)–15, about \(10^6\) configurations per point.

Results

At \(\lambda=0.03\), Binder curves for \(LT=6\)–15 cross near \(B_4\approx1.7\)–1.8, and the slope steepens with volume. Effective exponents \(y_t(\beta)\) from the derivative ratio behave very differently on the two sides:

  • \(\lambda=0.0125\) (triple-point side): \(y_t\) overshoots far above 3, to about 4.3 for \(LT=6,15\) and about 6 for \(LT=12,15\).
  • \(\lambda=0.0325\) (near tricritical): peaks at about 2.
  • \(\lambda=0.06\) (Ising side): peaks near 1.6, close to 1.587.
Reading the numbers

The overshoot \(y_t>3\) on the first-order side is not a physical exponent: the asymptotic first-order value is \(y_t=d=3\), so effective values above it are presumably pre-asymptotic finite-size behaviour of the derivative ratio near a strong first-order transition. The values are at \(N_t=4\) (coarse) in the hopping expansion, so \(\lambda_{\rm tric}\) is a lattice-parameter value, not yet a continuum quark mass. Work in preparation.

Summary

Ratios of Binder-cumulant derivatives extract critical exponents directly and control logarithmic corrections. Applied to the RW transition in heavy-quark QCD, they locate the tricritical point at \(\lambda_{\rm tric}\approx0.03\). Next steps: the chiral limit, locating the tricritical point with Lee–Yang zeros, and real chemical potential.

Q&A

Why use a derivative rather than comparing Binder values?

The Binder values at the endpoint (1.5, 1.604, 2) lie close together and drift with volume, whereas the slope ratio gives the exponent directly, and the candidate exponents (3, 2, 1.587) are well separated.

Is the tricritical Binder value exactly 2?

Only asymptotically. Three dimensions is the upper critical dimension for tricriticality, so logarithmic corrections make the approach slow; this is exactly what the \(\hat y_t\) term is designed to absorb.

Lee–Yang zeros and edge singularity in a finite-size effective model

MethodGyőző Kovács28 Oct, 15:55–16:25
Partition-function zeros3D IsingLandau model

A phase transition is a point where \(\ln Z\) stops being analytic, which can only happen where zeros of \(Z\) in the complex plane pinch the real axis. Tracking the leading zeros at finite volume gives a critical-point diagnostic that needs no order parameter, provided the fluctuation integral is kept, not replaced by its saddle point.

Why zeros

Background: Lee–Yang (added)

For the ferromagnetic Ising model, the Lee–Yang theorem puts all zeros in the complex magnetic field \(h\) on the imaginary axis. Above \(T_c\) they leave a gap: the Yang–Lee edge at \(h_{\rm YL}\propto i\,|t|^{\beta\delta}\). As \(T\to T_c\) the edge closes onto \(h=0\), which is the critical point seen from the complex plane.

Why the saddle point loses the zeros (added)

A saddle-point approximation replaces \(Z_V\) by a single exponential \(e^{-V f(\phi_*)}\), and an exponential is never zero. Zeros need at least two contributions that can cancel, such as the two ordered minima \(\pm\phi_*\) interfering at complex \(h\), or the full integral at the critical point. Keeping the integral keeps the interference.

Application to QCD

Real-\(\mu_B\) lattice simulations face the sign problem, so zeros are reconstructed from imaginary-\(\mu\) data or Taylor coefficients, typically with Padé approximants. In QCD the Ising fields \((t,h)\) are unknown linear combinations of \(T-T_c\) and \(\mu-\mu_c\), so the map must be fitted as well.

Systematics

Analytic continuation is ill-conditioned: truncated series and Padé fits can create spurious zeros or move real ones. Stability against truncation order, volume and fit ansatz has to be shown explicitly.

Takeaway

Lee–Yang zeros encode critical nonanalyticity directly; ratios of leading zeros give a universal finite-size handle, but only if volume dependence and analytic continuation are under control.

Q&A

Why ratios of leading zeros?

The common \(L^{-y_h}\) and the nonuniversal normalization cancel, leaving a cleaner universal signature than the position of any one zero.

Can imaginary-μ data locate the physical critical endpoint unambiguously?

No. Continuation and truncation are ill-conditioned; robust systematic checks are essential.

A syncretic model for the chiral phase transition

Proposal with testsRobert D. PisarskiBrookhaven National Laboratory28 Oct, 16:25–16:55
Fractional instantons’t Hooft determinantθ dependenceU(1)_AX(2370)

“Syncretic” means fusing two traditions, like the Gandhāra Buddha carved in Hellenistic style. The two traditions here are the Landau–Ginzburg–Wilson sigma model of chiral symmetry and large-\(N_c\) topology, in which the confined vacuum is a dense liquid of fractional instantons with \(Q=1/N_c\). Combining them puts fractional ’t Hooft determinants \((\det\Phi)^{n/3}\) into the effective theory. The talk tested this against lattice \(\theta\)-dependence, glueball masses at \(\theta\neq0\), \(U(1)_A\) probes and BESIII decays of the glueball candidate X(2370).

Beyond Landau at imaginary chemical potential

The opening result (Chen, Cherman, Pisarski, arXiv:2603.09977) uses ’t Hooft anomalies of QCD at imaginary baryon chemical potential \(i\mu_B\). In the plane of \(T\) and \(i\mu_B\), the chiral transition is argued to be beyond Landau: not describable by a Landau–Ginzburg–Wilson order-parameter theory in the Wilson–Fisher–Kadanoff sense. This is the caveat Philipsen referred to in the first talk of the session.

The standard sigma model

Large \(N_c\): why the vacuum needs \(Q=1/N_c\)

Take \(SU(N_c)\) with \(N_f\) massless flavours in the ’t Hooft limit (\(N_c\to\infty\), \(N_f/N_c\to0\)), and add \(\mathcal S\to\mathcal S+i\theta Q\). At \(N_c=\infty\) the theory is free, with \(O(1)\) glueball and meson masses, and the pure-glue topological susceptibility \(\chi\) is \(O(1)\) and \(T\)-independent below deconfinement. Quark loops are suppressed by \(1/N_c\), yet with massless quarks all \(\theta\)-dependence must cancel. Writing the \(p\to0\) topological correlator as a sum over glueball and meson poles, the cancellation requires one meson whose mass\(^2\) is \(O(1/N_c)\): the \(\eta'\).

Lattice evidence the speaker cited

On temperature dependence, lattice data at \(N=4,6\) (Del Debbio et al. 2004) show \(\chi(T)/\chi(0)\approx1\) below deconfinement and a sharp drop to nearly zero just above, sharper at larger \(N\). The speaker’s conclusion: topological charge is fractional below \(T_{\rm deconf}\) and integral above.

Alternative readings (added)

Standard large-\(N_c\) counting, with \(E(\theta)=N_c^2 f(\theta/N_c)\) and multiple branches, also predicts \(b_2\propto1/N_c^2\) without assuming any semiclassical objects, so the scaling alone does not single out fractional instantons; the coefficient is the more specific test. Likewise, the drop of \(\chi\) above \(T_c\) is expected from Debye screening of integer instantons (dilute-gas behaviour \(\chi\propto T^{-b}\)), so the claim “fractional below, integral above” is an interpretation of the change rather than a direct measurement of the charge.

Fractional determinants and the order of the transition

If the vacuum carries \(Q=1/3\) and \(2/3\) objects, the effective chiral Lagrangian contains \((\det\Phi)^{1/3}\) and \((\det\Phi)^{2/3}\) alongside \(\det\Phi\) (Pisarski–Rennecke 2024). In mean field, the transition is then second order if the expectation values of a tower of such operators vanish.

Power counting (editor’s reading)

\(\det\Phi\) has degree 3 in \(\Phi\) for \(N_f=3\); \((\det\Phi)^{q}\) has degree \(3q\), so \(q=\tfrac13,\tfrac23\) give linear-like and quadratic-like terms. Without the cubic invariant dominating, the mean-field drive toward a first-order transition is gone, which would be consistent with the lattice picture in Philipsen’s talk. Fractional powers are multivalued and nonanalytic at \(\Phi=0\), which is why the speaker writes them through the phase \(\det\Phi/|\det\Phi|\) below.

Above \(T_c\) the light condensate is small, but the ’t Hooft vertex couples \(u\), \(d\) and \(s\) together. Status

The ingredients (fractional charge with twisted boundary conditions, large-\(N_c\) \(\theta\)-scaling, X(2370) as a \(0^{-+}\) state) are established individually; their combination into one effective model is the proposal. Why should twisted-sector fractional topology affect ordinary QCD?

That is the central open question. Twisted boundary conditions show that \(Q=1/N_c\) objects exist mathematically; the speaker’s argument is that large-\(N_c\) consistency and the size of \(b_2\) require them in the physical vacuum.

Does \(b_2\propto1/N_c^2\) prove fractional instantons?

Could a nonpolynomial anomaly term change the universality class?

Potentially, but only once a consistent local effective description exists and its renormalization-group flow has been analysed.

IR phase & IR decoupling: their meaning & purposes

Contested claimIvan Horváth28 Oct, 16:55–17:25
IR phaseSpectral densityMobility edgeEffective dimension

With physical quark masses neither chiral nor center symmetry gives an exact order parameter, so the speaker proposed classifying thermal QCD by the infrared behaviour of the Dirac spectrum. Above a temperature \(T_{\rm IR}\) (claimed above the chiral crossover), near-zero modes pile up as \(\rho\sim1/\lambda\), become lower dimensional, and are separated from the bulk by a second mobility edge. Whether this is a genuine continuum phase is actively disputed. Philipsen’s talk earlier in the session cited this “third phase” (Alexandru–Horváth 2019) as one motivation for studying the conformal window.

Classifying by spectral density

Background: Banks–Casher (added) \[\lim_{m\to0}\lim_{V\to\infty}\langle\bar\psi\psi\rangle=-\pi\rho(0).\]

A nonzero density at \(\lambda=0\) is chiral symmetry breaking in spectral language. The order of limits is essential: the infinite volume comes first. Away from the chiral limit, \(\rho(0)\neq0\) is no longer an order parameter, which is why a spectral classification needs more than \(\rho(0)\).

The infrared spike and its mathematical caveat

How many dimensions does a mode fill?

The reported picture: extended 3D modes at low \(T\); above \(T_{\rm IR}\), near-zero modes with \(d_{\rm eff}\approx2\) and modes up to \(\lambda_A\) with \(d_{\rm eff}\approx0\); exact topological zero modes remaining roughly 3D.

Two mobility edges

Background: Anderson localization in QCD (added)

That low Dirac modes localize above the crossover, with a mobility edge \(\lambda_A\) that rises with \(T\), is well established (Giordano, Kovács, Pittler and others), and the transition at \(\lambda_A\) has been shown to be in the 3D unitary Anderson class. The new claim is a second, infrared boundary at \(\lambda=0\). Calling it Anderson-like critical requires the same tests: diverging localization length and level-spacing statistics interpolating between Poisson and random-matrix behaviour.

From spectra to gauge dynamics

The speaker argued that the IR power law signals a scale-invariant component of the glue, while the bulk retains ordinary QCD scales. A gluonic observable was expressed through overlap Dirac eigenmodes with a free-field subtraction, then split by a spectral cutoff into IR and bulk parts. The exact kernel was not in the transcript.

What a power law does not show

A power-law eigenvalue density alone does not establish scale-invariant correlators of local gauge-invariant operators, a vanishing trace anomaly, or a decoupled IR sector.

An analogy from disordered systems

A chiral random-hopping model with logarithmically correlated (scale-free) disorder shows the density-of-states exponent driven toward \(-1\) as the disorder cutoff grows, \(\rho(E)\propto|E|^{p(W)}\) with \(p(W)\to-1\). This proves such spectra can arise from scale-free disorder; it is an existence example, not a derivation of QCD.

Where would the transition be?

For pure glue the speaker associates the IR onset with deconfinement; for physical-mass QCD, with a separate line above the chiral crossover (\(T_{\rm IR}\) of order 200 MeV). A spectral nonanalyticity need not imply a thermodynamic one.

Status

A distinct IR phase with Anderson-like boundaries is an interesting but contested proposal. Measurements at two temperatures bracketing a change do not establish a discontinuity; that needs fine temperature resolution, infinite-volume scaling and a continuum limit.

Q&A

Does \(d_{\rm eff}\) really jump from 3 to 2?

A questioner argued intermediate temperatures could show a smooth evolution. The speaker cited results near 187 and 200 MeV bracketing the change, and agreed this does not prove a discontinuity between them.

Does the result survive the continuum limit, and does the probe operator matter?

The speaker noted that the probe operator need not match the sea-quark action, since it measures properties of the gauge ensemble. The audience stressed the need for a chiral probe (overlap) and controlled continuum behaviour; staggered-fermion work with collaborators was cited as support, without enough detail in the transcript to assess the extrapolation.

What would be decisive?

A nonanalytic infinite-volume continuum limit, ideally with independent scaling in gauge-invariant correlators or transport, beyond the spectral density itself.
